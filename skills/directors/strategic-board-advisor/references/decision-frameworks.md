# Decision Frameworks — Review, Risk & Growth Analysis

## Decision Review Protocol

For every major decision, run all seven questions before committing:

```
1. What are we assuming?
   List every assumption embedded in this decision. Make them explicit.
   Which assumptions are unverified? Which are verifiable but haven't been tested?

2. What could go wrong?
   Not worst-case fantasy — realistic failure modes.
   What has caused similar decisions to fail in other businesses?
   What internal factors could cause this to fail even if market conditions are favorable?

3. What are we not seeing?
   What information would change this decision if we had it?
   Who would disagree with this decision and why? (Steel-man their position.)
   What does the person most skeptical of this idea see that we don't?

4. What is the opportunity cost?
   If this consumes the next 90 days of capital and attention — what doesn't get done?
   Is what we're giving up worth less than what we're pursuing?
   Is this the best use of this resource, or just a good use?

5. What would invalidate this decision?
   What single event or piece of information would make this decision clearly wrong?
   How quickly would we know if this is failing? What's the feedback loop?

6. What is the downside?
   Worst realistic outcome. Not catastrophizing — honest.
   Can the business survive this downside? What is the recovery path?
   Is the downside acceptable given the upside?

7. What is the upside?
   Best realistic outcome. Not the pitch — the honest projection.
   How likely is the upside vs. the downside? What does the probability distribution look like?
```

**Output of decision review:**
Proceed / Proceed with modifications / Delay pending more information / Do not proceed

Always state which — and the single most important reason for the conclusion.

## Opportunity Cost Doctrine

**The most expensive decisions are the invisible ones.**

When evaluating any new initiative, map what it displaces:

```
OPPORTUNITY COST ANALYSIS:

New Initiative:           [Name]
Resources Required:       Capital: $[X] / Time: [X hours/week] / Attention: [Low/Medium/High]

What This Displaces:
  Current Priority 1:     [What gets less resource]
  Current Priority 2:     [What gets less resource]
  Founder Attention:      [What JorDache can no longer focus on]

The Real Question:
  Is [New Initiative] more valuable than the top of the current priority stack?
  → Yes: Proceed and formally deprioritize the displaced item
  → No: Do not proceed, regardless of how compelling the new initiative appears
  → Unclear: Do not proceed until clear
```

**Default position:** The burden of proof is on the new initiative.
Existing priorities earned their position. New initiatives must displace them — not add to them.

## Risk Evaluation Matrix

For every significant decision, assess across eight risk categories:

| Risk Type | Questions to Ask | Red Flags |
|---|---|---|
| Operational | Can we execute this reliably? Do we have the systems? | No clear owner, no defined process |
| Financial | Can we afford the downside? What's the cash exposure? | Requires capital from reserves |
| Brand | Could this damage trust, credibility, or positioning? | Association with low-credibility partners |
| Reputation | How would this look to investors, clients, top prospects? | Requires explaining away later |
| Execution | Is the timeline realistic given current load? | Multiple dependencies, optimistic scope |
| Market | Does this depend on conditions outside our control? | Relies on platform, algorithm, or third-party |
| Legal | Are there regulatory, compliance, or contractual risks? | Unclear jurisdiction, unvetted contracts |
| Dependency | Does this create reliance on a single person, platform, or client? | Single-point-of-failure structure |

**Risk scoring:** For each category, assess: Low / Medium / High
Any High rating requires explicit mitigation strategy before proceeding.
Two or more High ratings: do not proceed without board-level review.

## Growth Opportunity Assessment

Before pursuing any growth opportunity, assess all seven:

```
GROWTH ASSESSMENT:

Opportunity:          [Name]
Revenue Model:        [How it generates money]

Scalability:          Can this grow without proportional increase in cost or founder time?
                      Y / N / Limited — explain

Profitability:        What are the margins? When does it become profitable?
                      Timeline: [X months] / Margin at scale: [X]%

Complexity:           Does this add operational complexity to the ecosystem?
                      How many new systems, people, or dependencies does this require?

Defensibility:        Can this be easily copied by a competitor?
                      What creates durable advantage?

Long-Term Value:      Does this build an asset or generate transient income?
                      In five years: is this still valuable? More or less so?

Capital Requirements: How much capital is required to prove the concept? To scale?
                      Is this capital we have? Or would this require external funding?

Execution Requirements: What does this demand from JorDache personally?
                      Is that compatible with current priorities?
```

**Scoring:** An opportunity must score well on at least five of seven to warrant serious evaluation.
Scalability and Long-Term Value are the two most heavily weighted.

## Second-Order Thinking Protocol

Before any conclusion, ask: "And then what?"

Most people think one step ahead. Strategic advantage comes from thinking two or three.

**Template:**
```
Decision:             [X]
First-order effect:   [Immediate, obvious outcome]
Second-order effect:  [What happens as a result of the first-order effect]
Third-order effect:   [What happens as a result of the second-order effect]

Are the second and third-order effects desirable?
  If yes: proceed with confidence
  If unknown: proceed with monitoring
  If no: reconsider or redesign
```

**Example:**
Decision: Aggressively discount Operator Mentorship to fill seats
First-order: More clients, more revenue short-term
Second-order: Price anchors at the discounted rate, clients who paid full price are undervalued, brand positioning erodes
Third-order: High-ticket clients no longer perceive Blue Marlin and mentorship as premium — entire ecosystem positioning degrades

**Conclusion:** The second and third-order effects make this decision expensive regardless of short-term revenue gain.
